Issue #1 · week of September 8, 2026

Top 10 things in AI this week — for a business that has real work to finish

September 8, 2026 By Jed Vanden Bush Theos AI, Oconomowoc, Wisconsin

Every Monday I am going to write this the way I would tell it over coffee in Oconomowoc: what actually moved in AI last week, and what a regular business should do with it. Not the version for people who train models. The version for people who still have invoices to send, quotes to write, and a stack of follow-ups that did not go out on Friday.

I am not ranking this list by who shouted the loudest. I am ranking it by what I would care about if I ran a shop, a clinic, a contractor, or a five-person office around Lake Country. New models matter when they land in tools you already pay for. A privacy change matters if you handle client files. An outage matters the week it happens, because that is the week someone tried to finish a customer reply and found a blank screen. Big humanoid deals and factory videos matter later — they tell you the industry is serious, not that you should lease a robot this quarter.

This is Issue #1, for the week of September 8, 2026. I read the primary sources myself. Where a company used Claude, Copilot, ChatGPT, or an agent to do real work — notes, drafts, a production feed — I put that near the top. Where the story is mostly a press release or a science paper, it goes lower, with a Watch or Ignore so you do not waste a Tuesday on it. Read it in order if you want my ranking. Skip to the Try items if you only have ten minutes. The Ignore at the bottom is there on purpose.

If one of these sounds like the repetitive work holding your business back, we can look at it on a 30-minute call. No pitch deck. Just the constraint, and whether a 7-day sprint in the tools you already have is a fit. I would rather talk about the job that gets copied around all day than about a model name you will forget by Friday.

How I ranked this. Ordered by what matters to a local business owner this week: can you use it in tools you already have, does it change how you handle customer or client files, or is it a headline you can safely ignore until next year? Try means give it one real job. Watch means stay informed. Ignore means bookmark it and get back to work.

  1. 1

    GPT-6 Astra is rolling into ChatGPT for work

    What happened

    On September 3, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra and started rolling it out to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise, plus the API, Microsoft Azure, and AWS Bedrock. They call it their most capable and aligned model yet — stronger at computer use, browsing, and the professional work people already dump into ChatGPT. The chip headlines came later in the week. The business story landed first: the chatbot a lot of offices already open on Monday just got a new engine, in the same window, with the same login.

    Why you care

    You do not need a new vendor. If your team already uses ChatGPT for drafts or research, the tool took a step. What you need is a clearer job for it, and a person who still hits send. A smarter model that writes into the void is still a mess. A smarter model that takes the same brief you would give a junior hire — then gets reviewed — is a week you can feel.

    How people would use it

    Pick one repetitive writing job this week: a quote follow-up, a messy thread turned into a summary, or a first pass at a proposal from notes you already have. Run it through Astra with the same instructions you would say out loud. Keep the source files in your own folder so the chatbot is not the only copy. If the draft is wrong, fix the brief, not the whole stack. Give it the same two or three examples of a good answer you would give a new hire. Then read the thing before it leaves the building. That is the whole experiment. You will know in a week whether the new engine is actually faster for your shop, or just louder on the internet.

  2. 2

    Nationwide is using Copilot on the boring office work

    What happened

    On September 8, the UK building society Nationwide described rolling Microsoft 365 Copilot across the workforce while it stitches together Virgin Money. In finance, about 1,000 people with premium licenses use it to turn rough notes into first drafts, summarize meetings, shape messages, and pull actions. In an internal poll, 46 percent of finance respondents said they use it every working day. That is not a lab demo. That is people opening the Office suite they already had, on jobs that used to get retyped.

    Why you care

    You may not be merging a bank. You still have notes, meetings, and two lists that need to become one. Nationwide did not ask finance to learn a new app. They put Copilot on the work that gets copied around all day, then asked whether people actually used it. That last part matters. A license that nobody opens is just another tab.

    How people used it

    Rough notes become a first draft. A meeting becomes a short action list. A message gets shaped before it goes out. If you already live in Microsoft 365, give Copilot one meeting-notes-to-follow-up job for two weeks. Measure whether the follow-ups actually leave the building. If they do not, the tool is not the constraint. The habit is. I would rather you pick the Tuesday standup and the Friday customer list than “use Copilot more.” A named job is something you can check. A vibe is not.

  3. 3

    monday.com showed how they keep a production agent in the lane

    What happened

    On September 9, monday.com's engineering team published how they run feedAgent in production — an agent that curates each person's activity feed so the important work rises and the noise stays down. The lesson they keep repeating is the harness: strip private data before the model sees it, force a structured answer, drop made-up citations, cap how long a run can last, and evaluate the thing like software. They compare it to bowling bumpers. The ball can still miss. It should not go in the gutter.

    Why you care

    A lot of "AI agent" pitches skip the bumpers. monday.com is showing the version that ships: one job, tight inputs, a checklist after the model talks. The failures they name are the same ones that bite a local shop — a made-up fact, a runaway loop, a customer name the model should never have seen. That is why I put a production write-up this high. It is not flashy. It is usable.

    How people would use it

    Start with one inbox or one status report. Write the rules first: what it may read, what it must cite, what a person still approves. Do not let it email customers on day one. monday.com did not skip that layer. Neither should you. If you cannot explain the bumpers in two sentences, you are not ready for the agent. The useful version for a five-person shop is almost boring: one feed, one checklist, one person who still says yes. That is closer to a production harness than a weekend of “let’s try an agent.”

  4. 4

    Claude's new enterprise deal: your logs can stay in your cloud

    What happened

    On September 1, Anthropic announced Enterprise Frontier Safeguards, designed with more than 100 customers in banking, healthcare, law, and companies such as Comcast, KPMG, Mastercard, Salesforce, and Visa. Activity used for safety monitoring can live in your own Amazon S3, Azure Blob, or Google Cloud Storage, under your keys. Flags go to the customer's team, not to an Anthropic reviewer by default. It rolls out in phases this fall; eligible customers get zero data retention on Fable 5 and Fable 5.1 until then.

    Why you care

    If you handle client files, the question is no longer "is Claude smart?" It is "where do the logs live, and who can read them?" A local CPA, clinic, or law office should ask the same thing before pasting a customer file into any chatbot — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or the free tab someone opened at lunch. The big banks asked for custody of the logs. You can ask for a plain-English answer from whoever sold you the tool.

    How people would use it

    You do not need EFS this week. You do need a written answer: where are prompts stored, for how long, and who can open them? If the vendor cannot say, do not put client work in that tool yet. Use a cleaned-up example instead of the real file until you know. That is the Lake Country version of what the banks just negotiated. Put the answer in the same place you keep the Wi-Fi password — a note the next person can find — so it is not trapped in whoever took the sales call.

  5. 5

    Google dropped Gemini 3.8 Flash into Workspace

    What happened

    On September 2, Google released Gemini 3.8 Flash — its third Flash model in six weeks — aimed at agent work, coding, and multi-step reasoning. It is in Gemini Enterprise, the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers, AI Mode in Search, and Gemini in Google Sheets. A Cyber variant is limited to trusted defenders. Google is pushing the fast workhorse, not another giant Pro launch. For most offices, that is the model you actually open twice a day.

    Why you care

    If your business already lives in Gmail, Drive, and Sheets, this is the update that lands where you work. You do not have to migrate. The fast models are the ones you can use on invoices, quotes, and cleanup — not the ones that win a chart and then sit unused because they feel heavy. A shop that already pays for Google Workspace got a quieter upgrade than Astra, and that is fine. Quiet upgrades are how work gets done.

    How people would use it

    Open a messy spreadsheet and ask Gemini in Sheets to draft the cleanup steps: which columns to split, what looks like a duplicate, what should become a simple list. You stay in charge of the formulas. Same idea in Gmail or Drive: one cleanup job, one human check, then save the result in your own file. If the suggestion is wrong, you still have the original sheet. That is the whole point of a fast model in a tool you already trust — it should make the pile smaller, not become a second pile.

What I'm watching for Lake Country shops

If you run a shop, a clinic, or a five-person office from Oconomowoc to Hartland, the useful part of this week is not the humanoids. It is whether the tools you already open on Monday — ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Claude — can take one repetitive job off your plate without becoming the filing cabinet.

Ask where the logs live before you paste a client file. Keep a person on the send button. Save the finished draft in your own Drive or folder, because Thursday already proved the helper can vanish for an hour. And do not rebuild your software stack because a model got a new name. The constraint is still the work that gets copied around all day — not the brand on the chatbot.

If I were sitting with you this week, I would pick one job and leave the rest. Meeting notes to a follow-up list. A first pass at a quote. A messy sheet that needs a cleanup plan. Two weeks, not two hours. If that job still lives only in someone’s head, write it down first. The tool cannot fix a process you have not named.

    Thursday's outage: ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok all hiccuped

    What happened

    On the morning of September 3, ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok were down or erroring for a lot of people on the web and on iPhone. OpenAI's status page flagged ChatGPT and Codex. Anthropic reported elevated errors on Claude. They came back. The morning still happened. The same week those companies shipped new models, the old-fashioned lesson showed up first: the helper can vanish for an hour.

    Why you care

    If the only copy of a customer reply lives in a chatbot window, an outage is a closed shop. Helpers go down. Your filing cabinet should not. A local office that treats ChatGPT as the place work lives will feel that like a printer jam on Friday — except the jam is in someone else's building, and you cannot walk over and kick it. The same week the labs shipped new models, they also reminded you they do not owe you a Tuesday morning.

    How people would use it

    Draft in the chatbot if you want. Save the finished thing in your own inbox, Drive, or folder before you walk away. If a job cannot wait on a vendor's status page, it should not live only there. That is not anti-AI. That is how you stay open when the tab is blank. Write one sentence the person who answers the phone can follow: “If the helper is down, we use last week’s template and we call.” Stick it where the coffee lives. You do not need a disaster binder. You need a fallback the shop already agreed on.

    XPeng's IRON walked itself off the factory line

    What happened

    On September 8 in Guangzhou, XPeng said the first IRON humanoid finished automated final assembly and walked itself off the line. The company is aiming at scaled production by the end of 2026, first use in its own stores and campuses, and commercial deliveries in 2027. Three on-board Turing chips run the model on the robot so it does not need a remote operator for basic work. One robot leaving a line is not a fleet. It is a factory saying the line is on.

    Why you care

    A car company is treating a humanoid like a product that leaves a factory, not a stage demo. Still not something you buy for a Wisconsin shop this year. Fine to be curious. Ignore anyone selling "they're here next month." The useful question for a local owner is whether your own repetitive physical work is even written down — not whether to take a meeting with a robot salesperson. One robot off a line is a factory milestone. It is not a staffing plan.

    How people would use it

    You would not use IRON this week. You might write down the two most boring physical jobs in the building, so you have a list when 2027 stops being a slide and starts being a quote. Walking a box from A to B. Restocking the same shelf. The jobs that hurt because they are dull, not because they are skilled. If you never write them down, every demo will look like magic and none of them will match your floor.

    Nvidia's SONIC controller: tell a humanoid what to do in English

    What happened

    Researchers at Nvidia published SONIC in Science Robotics, covered this week on September 9. It is a controller — the robot's motor system — that turns VR, video, or language into whole-body motion, including moves it was not trained on. The next step they name is tying it to Nvidia Isaac GR00T so the robot can think and move in one loop. That is a research paper landing in a trade write-up, not a product page.

    Why you care

    This is how robots will eventually take "put that box on the shelf" as an instruction. It is not a product you can hire on Monday, and it will not quote a job for you. Unless you build robots for a living, bookmark it and get back to work. The English-in, motion-out idea is the interesting part. The buying decision is years away. I put it on the list so you can see why the Ignore exists: some weeks the paper is real, and it still is not your Tuesday.

    How people would use it

    You would not. Watch the official GR00T explainer if you like seeing where the industry is pointed — that is the stack SONIC is meant to plug into. Then close the tab. If a salesperson forwards you this paper as a reason to buy, ask when you can hire the thing. If the answer is a journal citation, you already have your answer.

  5. 10

    Jensen says AGI arrived. OpenAI's chief scientist says slow down.

    What happened

    On September 6, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang posted that Astra was trained on more than 100,000 Grace Blackwell chips and wrote "AGI has arrived," with 400,000 more GPUs coming next. The same day, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published "An Alien Mind": no lab has solved alignment well enough to keep flooring it, and he hopes voluntary slowdowns become normal until there are shared safety bars. The supplier and the scientist looked at the same model and told opposite stories.

    Why you care

    For a local business the takeaway is simpler than the word AGI. The tools are getting more powerful and less predictable. Keep a person in the loop on anything that touches a customer, a payment, or a medical or legal file. Use the new models. Do not believe a weekend post means your receptionist can go home. Caution from the lab is not a reason to freeze. It is a reason to keep the human on the send button. Jensen is selling chips. Pachocki is asking the labs not to floor it. You do not have to pick a camp. You have to decide who hits send.

    How people would use it

    Read the essay if you like the big picture. For Monday, the practice is the same as item 1: one real job, one review, one file that lives in your shop — not in a vendor's window. If the draft is about a customer, a payment, or a file you would be embarrassed to explain, a person still reads it. That is not fear. That is the same rule you already use for a new hire on week one.

A few questions I keep getting

Do I need to switch tools every time a new model drops?

No. I would stay in the suite you already pay for — ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, or Claude — and give the new model one repetitive job with a human on the send button. Switching tools every Monday is how projects stall. Astra, Copilot, and Gemini 3.8 Flash all landed inside products people already open. That is the point. Learn one job in the tool you have, then decide if a second tool is even a conversation. If you cannot name the job, you are not choosing a model. You are collecting tabs. A shop with six people does not need a bake-off. It needs one button the team already knows how to find, and a week of honest use on real work — a quote, a follow-up, a messy inbox — before anyone talks about migrating.

Pick the one workflow that gets copied, checked, and passed around all day. Meeting notes to a follow-up list is a good start. So is a first draft of a quote, or cleaning a messy spreadsheet. Write down what "done" looks like before you open the chatbot, and keep the finished file in your own Drive or folder — Thursday's outage is the reminder. Give it two weeks, not two hours.
